Your child is not giving you a hard time.

Sometimes your child is having a hard time.

Childhood behaviour is strongly influenced by developmental stage, temperament, communication skills, emotional regulation, environment and relationships.

A behaviour that is normal at two years may be concerning at twelve. Similarly, behaviour that looks "naughty" may sometimes be a sign that a child is tired, frustrated, anxious, overwhelmed or unable to communicate what they need.

The basic parenting principle:

Understand the reason → set the boundary → teach the alternative.

The goal of parenting is not to create a child who never cries, never argues and always obeys.

The goal is to gradually help your child develop:

  • Emotional regulation
  • Self-control
  • Confidence
  • Empathy
  • Responsibility
  • Independence
  • Healthy relationships
  • Resilience
  • Safe decision-making

Choose Your Child's Age

Parenting strategies should change as children develop. Start with your child's current developmental stage.

👶 0–3 Months Safety, bonding, crying and responsive care. 👶 3–6 Months Interaction, play, communication and routines. 👶 6–12 Months Exploration, separation anxiety and early boundaries. 🧒 1–2 Years Tantrums, biting, hitting, food and sleep. 🧒 2–3 Years Independence, discipline and emotional regulation. 👦 3–5 Years Aggression, lying, fears, school readiness and siblings. 👧 5–7 Years Rules, responsibility, friendships and school. 👦 7–10 Years Confidence, academics, bullying and responsibility. 🧑 10–12 Years Pre-adolescence, peers, puberty and digital life. 🧑 12–14 Years Puberty, emotions, privacy and independence. 🧑‍🎓 14–16 Years Relationships, social media and risk behaviour. 🧑‍🎓 16–18 Years Safe independence, relationships, money and future planning.

The Parenting Formula That Works Across Ages

For most everyday behavioural problems, try this sequence before resorting to shouting or punishment.

1. CONNECT Understand the emotion
2. SET LIMIT Clearly state the boundary
3. TEACH Show the alternative
4. REINFORCE Praise the desired behaviour
5. REPEAT Consistency matters
Remember:

Calm + Consistent + Connected generally works better than Strict + Angry + Punitive.

👶 0–3 Months: Building Safety & Connection

Your newborn does not need discipline. The main goals during this period are safety, responsive caregiving, feeding, sleep, comfort and developing a secure relationship with caregivers.

What does your baby need?

  • Safety
  • Responsive care
  • Appropriate feeding
  • Comfort and affection
  • Face-to-face interaction
  • Talking and singing
  • Appropriate sleep environment
  • Opportunities for gentle interaction

😭 "My baby cries a lot."

Crying is one of the major ways a young infant communicates. First consider common causes.

  • Hunger
  • Wet or soiled diaper
  • Temperature discomfort
  • Tiredness
  • Need for comfort
  • Overstimulation
  • Discomfort or illness
Think of crying as communication.

Instead of asking "How do I stop the crying?", first ask "What might my baby be trying to communicate?"

"Am I spoiling my baby by picking them up?"

Responding to a young infant's needs and providing comfort does not mean that you are spoiling the baby.

A helpful parent thought:

"My baby is communicating a need. My job is to understand the signal."

🗣️ Talk to your baby

Talk, sing, smile and interact face-to-face during everyday activities. Your baby does not need to understand every word for these interactions to be meaningful.

📖 Read to your baby

Simple picture books can be introduced from infancy. The objective is interaction and language exposure, not formal teaching.

📱 What about screens?

For a young infant, direct interaction with caregivers is much more valuable than passive screen exposure. Use talking, singing, facial expressions, cuddling and age-appropriate interaction instead of using a screen simply to occupy the baby.

🚩 When should crying concern you?

Seek medical assessment when unusual or persistent crying is accompanied by signs that the baby may be unwell, such as fever in a young infant, breathing difficulty, poor feeding, marked lethargy, repeated vomiting, seizures, injury or other concerning symptoms.

👶 3–6 Months: Interaction, Play & Communication

Your baby is becoming increasingly social and interactive. You may notice more smiling, vocalisation, interest in faces, movement and exploration.

🗣️ Talk to your baby

Narrate everyday activities.

"Now we are going to change your clothes."

"Here is your ball."

"Let's look at this picture."

🧸 Play

  • Peek-a-boo
  • Singing
  • Reaching for toys
  • Age-appropriate movement activities
  • Looking at faces
  • Copying sounds

📖 Reading

Use simple picture books and point to objects while naming them. The goal is shared interaction rather than academic learning.

"My baby wants to be held all the time."

Holding and responding to your baby is not bad parenting. Parents also need rest. It is reasonable to place a baby safely in an appropriate location for a short period while attending to essential needs.

Parenting priority at this age:

Talk → respond → play → read → comfort → repeat.

👶 6–12 Months: Exploration, Separation & Early Boundaries

This is the period when parenting begins to change significantly. Your baby may become mobile, explore everything, develop stronger preferences and become upset when you leave.

🚼 "My baby touches everything!"

Exploration is an important part of development. Instead of repeatedly saying "NO", make the environment safer.

Change the environment before trying to change the baby.

Move dangerous objects out of reach and create a safe area where your child can explore.

😟 Separation anxiety

Some babies become upset when their caregiver leaves. This can be part of normal development.

What can parents do?

  • Keep departures calm.
  • Use a predictable goodbye.
  • Avoid repeatedly returning solely because of crying if the baby is safe.
  • Avoid routinely disappearing secretly.
  • Gradually build successful experiences of separation.
Try saying:

"Mama is going now. Mama will come back."

🛑 Beginning of boundaries

You can start teaching very simple safety boundaries.

"Hot. Don't touch."
"That can hurt."
"We don't pull hair."

However, understanding a rule and consistently controlling behaviour are different skills. Your baby may understand your tone before being able to reliably control impulses.

The 6–12 month formula:

Protect the environment → Redirect unsafe behaviour → Use short words → Repeat consistently → Keep expectations developmentally appropriate.

🧒 1–2 Years: The Beginning of the Toddler Years

This is the age when many parents say:

"My child has suddenly become difficult."

Often the child has not become "bad". The child has discovered:

  • "I have preferences."
  • "I can say NO."
  • "I want to do it myself."
  • "I don't want to stop."

But the ability to regulate these emotions is still developing.

😡 Tantrums

Tantrums are common during the toddler years. Common triggers include hunger, tiredness, frustration, wanting independence, being told "no", difficulty communicating, waiting, transitions and overstimulation.

STEP 1 — Stay calm

Your child is already emotionally overwhelmed. Try to become the calmer adult in the room.

STEP 2 — Keep the child safe

If the child is hitting, biting, kicking, throwing dangerous objects or moving into danger, intervene.

STEP 3 — Use very few words

"I know you are angry."

"I won't let you hit."

"You can be angry, but you cannot hurt people."

STEP 4 — Don't accidentally reward the tantrum

If screaming consistently produces the desired object or activity, the behaviour can become harder to change.

The aim is not to win a power struggle. The aim is to avoid teaching that screaming is the most reliable way to obtain something.

STEP 5 — Reconnect after the child is calm

"You were very angry because I said no."

"Next time you can tell me what you want."

🛑 What NOT to do during a tantrum

  • Do not hit or slap.
  • Do not threaten the child.
  • Do not humiliate the child.
  • Do not call the child "bad".
  • Do not give lengthy lectures while the child is screaming.
  • Do not repeatedly change the rule because of the tantrum.

👊 "My toddler hits me."

First stop the behaviour. Gently block the hit or move the child away.

"I won't let you hit."

"You are angry."

"Use words. Say: I don't want it."

🦷 "My child bites."

Biting can occur because of frustration, limited communication, excitement, experimentation or other triggers.

Respond immediately but calmly:

"I won't let you bite. Biting hurts."

Do not bite the child back. Do not laugh. Do not turn the behaviour into a dramatic performance.

Look for the trigger

  • Was the child tired?
  • Hungry?
  • Frustrated?
  • Fighting over a toy?
  • Unable to communicate?
  • Overstimulated?

🙅 "My child says NO to everything."

Don't turn every request into a power struggle. Offer limited choices.

"Do you want to wear your shoes?"
"Do you want the blue shoes or the black shoes?"
"Will you come for your bath?"
"Do you want the red duck or yellow duck in the bath?"

Limited choices give the toddler some autonomy while keeping the important boundary with the parent.

🍽️ Food refusal

Toddlers commonly become selective about food.

Aim for:

  • Predictable meal structure
  • Calm meals
  • Reasonable portions
  • Repeated exposure to foods
  • Variety
  • Family meals where practical

Avoid turning every meal into a battle.

  • Don't force every bite.
  • Don't chase the child around with food.
  • Don't use screens as the automatic feeding solution.
  • Don't use sweets as the default bribe.
  • Don't repeatedly label the child as a "picky eater".
When to discuss feeding with your pediatrician:

Seek professional advice if there are concerns about poor growth, very restricted eating, recurrent choking, swallowing difficulties, nutritional deficiency or significant mealtime distress.

🛏️ Bedtime resistance

Toddlers may resist bedtime because they want more play, attention, control or proximity to parents.

Create a predictable routine.

Example:

Dinner → quiet play → bath → story → lights out

The exact routine can vary. The important part is predictability.

🚽 Toilet training

Avoid turning toilet training into a battle of wills. Look for readiness and teach gradually.

Encourage:

  • Recognising bodily signals
  • Sitting on the toilet or potty
  • Appropriate hygiene
  • Privacy
  • Hand washing

Accidents are part of learning. Avoid shaming the child.

🧒 2–3 Years: Independence, Boundaries & Emotional Regulation

The developmental theme of this age can be summarised as:

"I want to do it myself."

Expect strong preferences, repeated negotiation, "No!", emotional explosions and a growing desire for independence.

The objective is not perfect obedience. It is to teach:

  • Boundaries
  • Choices
  • Consequences
  • Emotional language
  • Self-control
  • Responsibility

The 2–3 year discipline toolkit

1. Positive attention

"I like how you waited for your turn."

2. Clear instructions

Instead of: "Don't make a mess."

Try: "Put the blocks in the box."

3. One instruction at a time

Young children may struggle when given a long list of instructions.

4. Limited choices

"Do you want to brush your teeth before or after the story?"

5. Redirection

Move the child's attention toward an acceptable activity when appropriate.

6. Logical consequences

Consequences should have a reasonable connection with the behaviour.

7. Consistency

The same behaviour should not receive completely different responses depending entirely on the parent's mood.

⭐ Catch them being good

Parents naturally notice misbehaviour. Try deliberately noticing appropriate behaviour.

"Stop shouting!"
"I like how quietly you asked me."
"Why did you throw your toys?"
"You put your toys back. Thank you."

❤️ Your emotional regulation matters too

Toddlers need adults who can help them regulate. If you feel yourself becoming extremely angry, pause.

If the child is safe:

  • Step back briefly.
  • Take several slow breaths.
  • Ask another caregiver to take over if available.
  • Return when calmer.
It is better to pause than to lose control.

🚩 When Should Parents Be More Concerned?

Difficult behaviour deserves closer attention when it is:

🔴
Persistent: continues despite reasonable and consistent parenting strategies.
🔴
Severe: unusually intense for the child's age.
🔴
Worsening: becoming progressively more difficult.
🔴
Affecting multiple settings: occurring at home, school and/or with peers.
🔴
Functionally impairing: interfering with school, friendships, sleep, eating or family life.
🔴
Developmentally unexpected: significantly outside what would generally be expected for the child's stage.
🔴
Associated with other concerns: developmental regression, significant anxiety, persistent sadness, severe sleep problems or significant social difficulties.

👨‍⚕️ When should you consult your pediatrician?

  • You are concerned about development.
  • You are struggling despite consistent parenting strategies.
  • Behaviour is affecting school or social functioning.
  • Aggression is severe or persistent.
  • There is regression of previously acquired skills.
  • Communication appears significantly delayed.
  • Eating or sleep problems are severe.
  • You suspect a developmental, emotional or behavioural disorder.
  • You simply feel that something is not right.
You don't have to wait for a problem to become severe.

Early discussion with a pediatrician can sometimes prevent a small problem from becoming a major family problem.

Medical Disclaimer

This guide is intended for general parent education and does not replace an individual consultation with a pediatrician or other qualified healthcare professional.

Children differ in temperament, development, family circumstances and individual needs. A behaviour described as common does not necessarily mean that it is appropriate in every child.
