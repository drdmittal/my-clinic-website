Child safety

Keeping your child safe at home, on the road and at school

Most injuries in children happen at home, in a few seconds, in front of a caring adult, and most of them can be prevented. Choose your child’s age to see what to do at home, on the road and at school, and what your child can learn at that age.

By Dr. Dinesh Mittal, MBBS, DCH · Delhi Medical Council Reg. No. DMC/R/00668

Choose your child’s age
0–1 year

Baby

A baby cannot move away from danger, so every safety step is yours. Babies also learn new skills without warning: rolling at about 4 months, grabbing and mouthing at 5–6 months, crawling at 8–9 months, and pulling up to stand soon after. Stay one step ahead of the next skill.

Home safety

Sleep

  • Put your baby to sleep on the back, on a firm, flat surface of their own, next to your bed.
  • Keep pillows, quilts, bolsters and soft toys away from the baby’s face.
  • No cords, chains or strings around the neck. Keep curtain cords and jhoola ropes out of reach.
  • If you use a cloth cradle (jhoola), keep it low and stable, and never leave the baby in it unattended.

Falls

  • Never leave a baby alone on a bed, sofa, table or changing surface, even for a moment. Keep one hand on the baby.
  • Do not use a baby walker. Walkers cause falls down stairs and let a baby reach hot and sharp things. They do not help a baby walk sooner.
  • Always fasten the straps of a pram, high chair or bouncer.

Burns and hot things

  • Do not hold your baby while you drink tea, carry hot food or cook.
  • Test bath water with your elbow or the inside of your wrist before every bath.
  • Keep room heaters, irons and hot water bottles out of reach.
  • Never burn a coal angithi or run a gas heater in a closed room. The fumes can kill silently. Keep a window slightly open.

Water

  • Never leave a baby alone in or near water, not even to answer the phone or the doorbell. Take the baby with you.
  • Empty buckets and tubs straight after use.

Choking and swallowing

  • Keep coins, buttons, beads, safety pins, bindis and button batteries out of reach. Anything that fits inside a toilet-roll tube is too small for a baby.
  • Once solid food starts: feed with the baby sitting upright, and stay with the baby while eating.
  • No whole nuts, whole grapes, whole chana or popcorn. Mash food soft.

Handling

  • Never shake a baby. If the crying overwhelms you, lay the baby down safely on the back, step away for a few minutes, and ask someone to help.
  • Never leave a baby alone with a young child or a pet.
  • Give medicines only as prescribed, measured with the syringe or dropper that comes with the medicine.
Road and travel

In a car

  • The safest place is a rear-facing baby car seat, fixed on the back seat. Keep your baby rear-facing for as long as the seat allows.
  • A lap is not safe. In a crash, an adult cannot hold on to a baby.
  • Never place a rear-facing seat on a front seat that has an airbag.
  • Never leave a baby alone in a parked car, even for a minute.

Two-wheelers, autos and prams

  • A scooter or motorcycle is not a safe way for a baby to travel. Use a car, cab or auto when you can.
  • Indian rules require a safety harness and a helmet for children from 9 months to 4 years on a motorcycle, and a speed of 40 km/h or less.
  • In an auto or e-rickshaw, sit in the middle with the baby held close, away from the open sides.
  • On the road, keep the pram’s brake on whenever you stop, and do not hang heavy bags on the handle.
Outside the home and other carers

People who look after your baby

  • Leave your baby only with an adult you trust, who knows these safety rules.
  • Write your phone numbers and the doctor’s number where the carer can see them.
  • Show grandparents and helpers how the baby sleeps, and what the baby must not be given.

Crèche or day care

  • Check that babies sleep on their backs on separate, flat surfaces.
  • Check that there are enough carers, that stairs and kitchen are closed off, and that only people you have named can collect your baby.

Outings and festivals

  • Keep babies well away from firecrackers, diyas, Holi colours and loud speakers.
  • In crowds, carry the baby close to your body.

Parents of a baby often ask

Is a baby walker safe?

No. A walker lets a baby move fast towards stairs, hot liquids and sharp edges, and babies in walkers are injured every year. Walkers do not help a baby walk earlier. Time on the floor, pulling up on furniture and walking while holding your hands are safer.

Can my baby sleep in our bed?

The safest place is the baby’s own firm, flat surface, placed next to your bed. An adult bed has pillows, quilts and gaps, and a sleeping adult can roll towards the baby. Keeping the baby beside your bed lets you feed and comfort easily.

My baby rolled off the bed. What should I do?

Pick the baby up and stay calm. Go to a hospital at once if the baby was unconscious even briefly, has a fit, vomits more than once, is unusually sleepy, will not feed, or has bleeding from the ear or nose or a soft swelling on the head. Even if the baby seems fine, a bump on the head should be seen by a doctor the same day.

Can I hold my baby in my lap in the car?

No. In a crash or sudden braking, the force is far greater than an adult’s arms can hold. A rear-facing baby car seat on the back seat is the safe way.

1–3 years

Toddler

Toddlers walk, climb, open and taste everything, but they do not understand danger. Telling a toddler “no” is not enough. Make the home safe, and stay within arm’s reach near water, roads and heights.

Home safety

Electricity

  • Fit safety covers on every socket the child can reach.
  • Keep extension boards, chargers and loose wires off the floor.
  • Never leave an immersion rod, iron or geyser switched on with a child nearby.
  • Plug mosquito-repellent machines high up, out of reach.

Beds, stairs, balconies and windows

  • Fit guard rails on the bed, or keep the mattress on the floor.
  • Fit safety gates at the top and the bottom of the stairs. Keep the stairs clear and well lit.
  • Fit grills on windows and balconies. Gaps in railings should be narrow enough that a child cannot pass through (about 10 cm or less).
  • Keep chairs, stools, coolers and flower pots away from railings and windows. Toddlers use them to climb.
  • Keep the terrace door locked.

Medicines

  • Keep all medicines under lock and key, high up. This includes grandparents’ tablets for blood pressure, sugar and heart, and iron tablets.
  • Never call a medicine “toffee” or “sweet”.
  • Keep medicines in their original packs. Do not leave strips in handbags, on bedside tables or on the dining table.

Toilet cleaners and other chemicals

  • Lock away toilet cleaner, phenyl, bleach, drain cleaner, kerosene, pesticide, rat poison, naphthalene balls, camphor, mosquito liquid, hair dye and nail-polish remover.
  • Never store kerosene, phenyl or acid in a water or cold-drink bottle. A child will drink from it.
  • Keep the puja diya, matchbox, agarbatti and camphor (kapoor) out of reach. Swallowed camphor can cause fits.

Kitchen and hot water

  • Keep toddlers out of the kitchen while you cook. A safety gate at the kitchen door helps.
  • Use the back burners, and turn pan handles inward.
  • Keep hot tea, dal and milk away from the edge of the table. Do not use a tablecloth that a child can pull.
  • For a bucket bath, pour cold water first and then add hot. Never leave a bucket of hot water on the bathroom floor.
  • Keep the matchbox, lighter, knives and the pressure cooker out of reach.

Water

  • A toddler can drown silently in a few centimetres of water. Empty buckets and tubs after use.
  • Keep the bathroom door closed and the toilet lid down.
  • Keep the lids of water tanks and underground tanks closed and locked.

Choking and swallowing

  • Keep coins, button batteries, magnets, beads and small toy parts out of reach. Check remotes, watches and musical toys: the battery cover must be screwed shut.
  • No whole nuts, whole grapes, whole chana, popcorn or hard toffees before 4 years. Cut grapes into four.
  • Feed only when the child is sitting. No eating while running, playing, laughing or lying down.
  • Keep plastic bags and burst balloon pieces away.

Furniture, doors and animals

  • Fix the TV, almirah and bookshelves to the wall so they cannot tip over.
  • Use corner guards on sharp edges, and door stoppers to protect fingers.
  • Use bathroom latches that can be opened from outside.
  • Never leave a toddler alone with a dog, including the family pet.
Road and travel

Walking

  • Hold your child’s hand at all times near a road. Keep the child on the side away from traffic.
  • Never let a toddler play near parked cars or in the driveway. Before reversing, walk behind the car and look.
  • In lifts and on escalators, hold the child’s hand and keep feet away from the edges.

In a car

  • Use a child car seat with a harness, on the back seat. Keep it rear-facing for as long as the seat allows.
  • Switch on the child lock for doors and windows.
  • Never leave a child alone in a car, even with the engine and AC running.

On a two-wheeler

  • Avoid it when you can. If you must, the law requires a helmet and a safety harness for children from 9 months to 4 years, and a speed of 40 km/h or less.
  • Never let a child stand in front on a scooter.
  • On a cycle or motorcycle, keep the child’s feet away from the wheel. Use a child seat with foot guards.
Play school, parks and outings

Play school or day care

  • Check that gates stay closed, stairs are guarded, and the kitchen and cleaning store are locked.
  • Check that staff are verified, and that the child is handed over only to people you have named.
  • Ask who gives first aid, and how you will be informed of any injury.
  • For a school van: an attendant, a seat for every child, and doors that stay closed.

Parks and playgrounds

  • Use swings and slides made for small children, on a soft surface, with you within arm’s reach.
  • In summer, touch a metal slide before use. It can burn.
  • Near a pool, pond or fountain, stay close enough to touch the child at all times.

Markets, crowds and festivals

  • Keep a card with your phone number in the child’s pocket. Take a photo of the child that day, in the clothes worn.
  • Diwali: no crackers in a toddler’s hands, and keep diyas above reach.
  • Holi: protect the eyes, and use safe colours only.
  • Kite season: manjha can cut the neck. Keep small children off two-wheelers during kite-flying days.
What your toddler can learn

Teach and repeat every day

  • The words “hot”, “sharp”, “stop” and “no”.
  • To hold your hand on the road and in the market.
  • To give you anything found on the floor, and not to put it in the mouth.
  • To sit down while eating.

Parents of a toddler often ask

My child has swallowed a coin or a battery. What should I do?

A button battery or a magnet is an emergency: go to a hospital at once, even if your child looks well, and do not make the child vomit. For a coin, see a doctor the same day; go to a hospital at once if there is drooling, vomiting, chest pain or trouble breathing or swallowing.

My toddler drank from a bottle of phenyl, kerosene or cleaner. What now?

Do not make the child vomit, and do not give anything to “wash it down”. Take the bottle with you and go to the nearest hospital now. On the way, you can call the AIIMS poison line on 1800 116 117.

I watch my child all the time. Do I still need gates, grills and socket covers?

Yes. No one can watch a toddler every second, and injuries happen in the few seconds when the phone rings or the doorbell goes. Safety fittings protect your child in those seconds.

We have only a scooter. How do I carry my child safely?

Carry one child only. Use a helmet that fits the child, with the strap fastened. For children from 9 months to 4 years, use a safety harness that ties the child to the rider, and keep the speed at 40 km/h or less. Never let the child stand in front, and keep the feet away from the wheel.

3–5 years

Preschool child

Preschool children are fast, curious and keen to copy adults. They can learn simple safety rules, but they forget them when excited. Keep the home safe as for a toddler, and start teaching.

Home safety

Keep from the toddler stage

  • Medicines and chemicals stay locked. Socket covers, window grills and stair safety stay in place.
  • Hot food, hot water, the kitchen and the bucket rule stay the same.

New risks at this age

  • Keep matches, lighters, scissors, knives, blades and tools out of reach.
  • Children hide in almirahs, car boots, washing machines and empty water tanks. Keep them closed or locked.
  • Remove low inside latches, or keep a key outside, so a child cannot get locked in a bathroom or room.
  • A child must not use the lift alone.
  • Teach your child never to touch switches or plugs with wet hands.

Water

  • Stay within arm’s reach in or near any water: a pool, a pond, a water park or a large tub.
  • Armbands and tubes are toys, not safety equipment.

Dogs and other animals

  • Teach your child not to disturb a dog that is eating, sleeping or with puppies, and not to run from a street dog. Stand still and look away.
  • Any bite, scratch, or lick on broken skin: wash with soap and running water for 15 minutes, and see a doctor the same day.
Road and travel

Walking

  • Hold hands near every road. A child of this age must never cross alone.
  • Start teaching: stop at the edge, look right, look left, look right again, and listen.
  • Use the footpath, the zebra crossing and the foot overbridge every time, so your child copies you.

Car and two-wheeler

  • Use a car seat with a harness or a booster seat, on the back seat.
  • On a two-wheeler, every child above 4 years must wear a helmet that fits, with the strap fastened. This is the law.
  • Only one child on a two-wheeler, seated behind the rider, holding on.

Cycles and scooters

  • A helmet for every ride, even in the park.
  • Tricycles, cycles and kick-scooters are for the park or a closed lane, not the road.
School and outings

School bus or van

  • Check for a seat for every child, an attendant, closed doors, and no overcrowding.
  • Teach your child to wait away from the road, to board only when the bus has stopped, and never to walk behind the bus.
  • Hand over and collect your child yourself, or through a person the school knows.

At school

  • Give the school your phone numbers, the names of people allowed to collect your child, and details of any allergy or illness.
  • Agree on a family password. Your child goes with no one who does not know it.
  • Pack food that is easy to chew. No whole nuts or hard toffees in the tiffin.

Outings

  • In a crowd, dress your child in bright clothes and keep your phone number in the pocket.
  • Tell your child what to do if lost: stay where you are, and ask a shopkeeper, a guard or a mother with children for help.
What your child can learn

By 5 years, teach your child

  • Their full name, your names, and your mobile number.
  • Never to go anywhere with anyone without asking you first.
  • Never to open the door of the house.
  • That the parts covered by underwear are private, and to tell you about any touch that feels wrong.
  • To tell an adult at once about any injury, or about anything swallowed.

Parents of a preschool child often ask

When should I start teaching body safety?

From about 3 years, in simple words: the parts covered by underwear are private, no one should touch them, we do not keep secrets from Mummy and Papa, and you can always tell us. Repeat it calmly, the way you teach road rules. The “My Body Belongs to Me” game on this website can help.

How do I prepare my child for getting lost in a market?

Teach your child their full name and your mobile number. Agree on the rule: stay where you are, and ask a shopkeeper, a guard or a mother with children for help. In crowds, keep your number in the child’s pocket and take a photo of the child that day.

A street dog scratched my child, but there is no bleeding. Do we need to see a doctor?

Yes. Wash the area with soap and running water for 15 minutes, and see a doctor the same day. The doctor will decide about anti-rabies vaccine. Do not wait to watch the dog.

How do I know the school van is safe?

Check for a seat for every child, an attendant other than the driver, doors that stay closed, and no overcrowding. Know the driver’s name and number, and tell the school at once if your child reports rash driving.

6–12 years

School-age child

School-age children spend more time away from you: at school, in the lane, in the park and online. Safety now depends on rules that you agree together and repeat often, and on your own example.

Home safety

Kitchen, fire and electricity

  • Teach safe use of the gas stove, knife and microwave, and allow it only with an adult present.
  • No firecrackers without an adult. Keep a bucket of water nearby, and wear cotton clothes.
  • Teach your child what to do if there is a gas smell: do not touch any switch, open the windows, go outside, and tell an adult.
  • Medicines stay locked. A child takes no medicine without an adult.

Terrace, lift and play

  • No kite flying or play on a terrace without a high wall. Falls from terraces are a common cause of serious injury.
  • No leaning on balcony railings, and no climbing on water tanks.
  • Teach your child to step back if a lift door is closing, and never to force it open.

When your child is alone at home

  • Young children should not be left alone at home.
  • For an older child left for a short time, set clear rules: the door stays closed to everyone, no cooking, and your phone number is written near the phone.

Phone and internet

  • Keep phones, tablets and computers in a shared room, not behind a closed door.
  • Rules: no name of school, address, photos or OTP shared with anyone online. No chatting with strangers in games.
  • Tell your child: “If anything online scares or upsets you, tell me. You will not be punished.”

Water and animals

  • Teach your child to swim. Even a good swimmer never swims alone, and never in a river, canal or pond.
  • Any animal bite or scratch: wash with soap and running water for 15 minutes, and see a doctor the same day.
Road and travel

Walking

  • Most children cannot judge the speed and distance of traffic until about 10 years. Until then, they cross with an adult.
  • Use the zebra crossing or foot overbridge. Stop, look right, look left, look right again, and walk, do not run.
  • No phone and no earphones while walking on the road.
  • On a metro platform, stand behind the yellow line.

Cycling

  • A helmet for every ride. Check the brakes, bell, light and reflectors.
  • Ride on quiet lanes in daylight, keeping to the left. No riding on main roads.

Car, two-wheeler and school bus

  • Back seat, with a seat belt, on every trip. Use a booster seat until the belt fits across the chest and hips, which is usually at about 145 cm of height (8–12 years).
  • Children sit in the front seat only after 13 years.
  • On a two-wheeler: a helmet that fits, strap fastened, one child only.
  • A child must never drive a scooter or motorcycle.
  • School bus: stay seated, keep head and hands inside, and cross only where the driver can see you.
School safety

Every day

  • Your child should know two phone numbers by heart, and carry an identity card.
  • The school bag should weigh no more than about one-tenth of your child’s weight. Use both shoulder straps.
  • Send a water bottle. On very hot days, send a cap as well.
  • On days of severe air pollution, follow the school’s advice on outdoor play.

Health needs

  • If your child has asthma, a food allergy, fits or diabetes, give the class teacher a written plan and the medicine or inhaler, and renew it every year.

Bullying and body safety

  • Ask about the day, every day. Watch for a child who does not want to go to school, loses things often, or comes home hungry or hurt.
  • Teach your child to tell you or a teacher about bullying. Take it up with the school, in writing if needed.
  • Repeat the body safety rules: say no, get away, and tell a trusted adult.

Trips, sports and coaching

  • Give written consent only when you know who is going, how they will travel, and who is in charge.
  • Sports: the right shoes and guards, a warm-up, and water breaks.
  • Your child goes home only with the people you have named.
What your child can learn

By 12 years, teach your child

  • The emergency number 112, their home address, and two phone numbers.
  • Simple first aid: cool a burn under tap water, press on a cut with a clean cloth, and call an adult.
  • To say no to a dare.
  • To tell you about secrets that feel wrong.

Parents of a school-age child often ask

When can my child cross the road alone?

Most children cannot judge the speed and distance of traffic until about 10 years. Before that, cross together and let your child lead while you watch. After that, start with quiet roads you have practised on.

At what age can my child sit in the front seat of the car?

After 13 years. Until then, the back seat with a seat belt, and a booster seat for a smaller child, is the safest place.

From what age can my child stay at home alone?

There is no single right age. It depends on how responsible your child is, how long you will be away, and whether a trusted neighbour is nearby. Start with a few minutes, set clear rules about the door, the kitchen and the phone, and check that your child can reach you.

My child is being bullied at school. What should I do?

Listen without blaming, and thank your child for telling you. Write down what happened, when and who was there. Meet the class teacher, and put it in writing if it continues. Do not tell your child to hit back. Watch for sadness, poor sleep or refusing school, and see your doctor if these appear.

13–18 years

Teenager

Teenagers want freedom, and they take more risks when friends are watching. Rules work best when you explain the reason, agree on them together, and keep them yourself.

Home safety

Kitchen, gas and appliances

  • Teach your teenager to cook safely, and to switch off the gas regulator after use.
  • Gas leak: do not touch any switch, open the doors and windows, turn off the regulator, go outside, and call 1906.
  • Geyser and immersion rod: switch off before touching the water.

Medicines and substances

  • No medicine without your knowledge. No sharing of medicines with friends.
  • Talk early and openly about tobacco, vapes, alcohol and drugs. Ask what they have seen among friends, and listen before you advise.
  • Energy drinks are not for teenagers. Gym supplements only on a doctor’s advice.

Online safety

  • Agree on rules for privacy settings, strangers, photos and money. Never share an OTP.
  • If someone threatens to share a photo or asks for money, your teenager should stop replying, keep the messages, and tell you. Report on 1930.
  • Keep phones out of the bedroom at night.

Mood and stress

  • Notice changes: a teenager who withdraws, stops eating or sleeping well, or loses interest in friends and school needs help.
  • Listen first, without lecturing. Ask your doctor for help. Tele-MANAS, on 14416, is free at any hour.
  • If your teenager seems very low, keep medicines and chemicals locked away.
Road and travel

Driving

  • No scooter, motorcycle or car before the legal age and a licence. Under Indian law, the parent or the vehicle owner is held responsible when a minor drives.
  • Never ride with a driver who is under age, has been drinking, or is showing off.
  • Make this promise: “Call me from anywhere, at any hour. I will come and get you, and we will talk the next day.”

Helmets, seat belts and phones

  • A helmet on every two-wheeler ride, as rider or pillion, with the strap fastened.
  • A seat belt on every car trip, including on the back seat.
  • No phone and no earphones while cycling, crossing a road or walking beside traffic.

Cabs, metro and late evenings

  • Check the number plate before getting into a cab, sit in the back, and share the trip with you.
  • For coaching and late classes, agree on a route and a time, and share live location.
School, friends and outings

School and coaching

  • Bullying and online bullying: keep the messages as proof, and take it up with the school.
  • Exam stress: a study plan, regular sleep and daily play protect a teenager better than extra hours of study.
  • Sports and gym: learn the right technique from a trained coach, and drink water.

Outings, trips and parties

  • You should know where, with whom, and the time of return. This is safety, not suspicion.
  • Never swim in a river, canal, lake or quarry on a picnic, and never after dark.
  • No selfies at the edge of a terrace, a railway track, a river or a moving vehicle.
  • Take a drink only if you have opened or poured it yourself.

Relationships and body safety

  • Teach that no one has the right to touch, photograph or pressure your teenager, and that your teenager must respect the same in others.
  • Keep the door open: “You can tell me anything. I will listen first.”
What your teenager can learn

Before 18, your teenager should know

  • Emergency numbers: 112 for any emergency, 1906 for a gas leak, 1930 for online fraud.
  • First aid for burns, bleeding, nosebleeds and fits.
  • How to say no to friends without losing face: “My parents will find out,” or “I have a match tomorrow.”

Parents of a teenager often ask

My 16-year-old wants to ride a scooter to coaching. Can I allow it?

Not without a licence. The law allows a licence for ordinary scooters and motorcycles only at 18 years. At 16, only a motorcycle of up to 50 cc is permitted, and ordinary scooters are bigger than that. When a minor drives, the parent or vehicle owner is held responsible. A cycle, the metro, a shared cab or a lift from you is the safer choice.

My teenager refuses to wear a helmet. What can I do?

Make it a fixed family rule: no helmet, no ride, for everyone, including you. Buy a helmet that fits well and that your teenager has chosen. Explain the reason once, calmly, and then hold the rule without arguing each time.

Should I track my teenager’s phone and location?

Agree on it openly. Sharing live location during travel and late classes is a safety habit for the whole family, parents included. Secret checking breaks trust, and a teenager who fears punishment stops telling you things.

How do I know if my teenager needs help for low mood?

Get help if, for two weeks or more, your teenager is sad or irritable most days, has lost interest in friends and activities, sleeps or eats very differently, or marks fall suddenly. Talk to your doctor. If your teenager speaks of not wanting to live or of self-harm, stay with them and seek medical help the same day. Tele-MANAS on 14416 is free at any hour.

Seek urgent medical attention

Call 112 or go to the nearest hospital now if your child has any of these. Do not wait for a clinic appointment.

  • Swallowed a button battery, a magnet, a medicine, a cleaner, kerosene or any chemical. Do not make the child vomit. Take the container or pack with you.
  • Choking, with trouble breathing, a weak cry or cough, or blue lips.
  • Pulled out of water after going under, even if the child seems fine afterwards.
  • A burn larger than the child’s palm, or on the face, hands, feet or private parts, or from electricity or a chemical.
  • A fall or head injury followed by unconsciousness, a fit, repeated vomiting, unusual sleepiness, or bleeding from the ear or nose.
  • An electric shock.
  • A deep cut, or bleeding that does not stop after 10 minutes of firm pressure.
  • Smoke or fumes breathed in, followed by cough, breathlessness or drowsiness.
