Dengue in Children in Delhi: Symptoms, Prevention & When to Rush to a Doctor

Dr. Dinesh Mittal, MBBS, DCH
Child Specialist & Pediatrician | Sector 3 Rohini, Delhi

Every year, as the monsoon settles over Delhi, hospitals and pediatric clinics across the city see a sharp rise in fever cases — and a significant number of them turn out to be dengue. In recent years, pediatricians in Delhi have also noted that dengue transmission is no longer confined strictly to the traditional July–November window; cases are now being diagnosed earlier in the year, which means parents must maintain vigilance for longer.

Children are uniquely vulnerable to dengue. Their smaller body mass, developing immune systems, and difficulty in clearly describing symptoms like retro-orbital pain (behind the eyes) or dizziness make it harder for parents to catch early warning signs. This guide breaks down what dengue looks like in children, how to manage home recovery safely, and the critical red-flag signs that require immediate hospital care.

Why Delhi Sees a Dengue Spike Every Year

Dengue is transmitted through the bite of the Aedes aegypti mosquito, which breeds in clean, stagnant water — desert coolers, flowerpot trays, uncovered overhead water tanks, and construction sites are common culprits across Delhi NCR. This mosquito is a daytime biter, most active in the early morning and late afternoon, coinciding exactly with when school children are playing outdoors or commuting.

Municipal health data shows cases accumulating steadily through the monsoon rains and peaking during the post-monsoon months of October and November. When rain-induced stagnant puddles meet warm temperatures, mosquito-breeding cycles accelerate, making household-level prevention crucial.

Common Symptoms of Dengue in Children

Dengue often begins like a standard childhood viral illness. Watch closely for:

  • Sudden high-grade fever (often 102°F – 104°F) lasting 4 to 7 days.
  • Severe headache, especially throbbing pain behind the eyes.
  • Intense body ache and joint pain (often termed "breakbone fever"); younger toddlers may present as unusually irritable or refusing to walk/crawl.
  • Nausea, persistent vomiting, or complete loss of appetite.
  • Skin rash: Flat, reddish spots appearing 2 to 5 days into the fever.
  • Unusual tiredness, extreme lethargy, or inconsolable crying in toddlers who cannot express their discomfort verbally.
  • Abdominal tenderness or stomach ache.

The Platelet Question: What Parents Should Actually Know

Clinical Facts on Platelet Monitoring:

A normal platelet count ranges between 1.5 lakh and 4.5 lakh per microlitre of blood. Pediatricians use serial (repeated) CBC blood tests to evaluate the overall trend rather than reacting to an isolated value.

  • A falling platelet count alone does not automatically signify severe dengue: Doctors assess platelets in conjunction with hematocrit levels, hydration status, pulse pressure, and overall alertness.
  • Platelet transfusion is NOT needed for every child with dengue: Transfusions are strictly reserved for rare situations involving profound thrombocytopenia combined with active clinical bleeding. The decision is always made by the treating pediatrician based on the child's complete clinical condition — never by a laboratory number alone.

Emergency Red Flags: When to Rush to a Doctor Immediately

The most critical phase of dengue often begins just as the fever starts to subside (Days 3 to 7). Take your child to the nearest hospital or emergency room immediately if you observe:

  • Severe, continuous, or worsening abdominal pain.
  • Repeated vomiting (more than 3 episodes within 24 hours).
  • Bleeding from the nose, bleeding gums, or dark/black stool.
  • Cold, clammy hands and feet, or pale/bluish skin.
  • Rapid breathing, grunting, or difficulty catching breath.
  • Extreme lethargy, confusion, or difficulty waking up.
  • Significantly reduced urination (no wet diaper or urine for over 6 hours).
  • A sudden drop in temperature accompanied by severe weakness and sweating.

Prevention: Protecting Your Child at Home & School

Since there is no specific antiviral medication for dengue, vector control and bite prevention are your family's strongest defenses:

Around the House

  • Empty, scrub, and dry desert coolers, flowerpot trays, and pet bowls at least once every week.
  • Ensure overhead water tanks and plastic storage drums are sealed with tight lids.
  • Clear discarded tires, plastic containers, and debris from balconies and rooftops.
  • Install fine wire mesh screens on windows and balcony doors.

For Your Child Directly

  • Dress children in loose, light-colored, full-sleeved shirts and full-length trousers, especially during morning and dusk play.
  • Apply pediatrician-approved mosquito repellents containing DEET, Picaridin, or Oil of Lemon Eucalyptus (PMD) on exposed skin as per product guidelines.
  • Use fine mosquito bed nets for infants and toddlers during daytime afternoon naps.

Home Care If Your Child Is Diagnosed with Dengue

For mild cases managed at home under pediatric supervision, supportive care revolves around maintaining adequate fluid balance:

  • Frequent Oral Hydration: Offer small, continuous sips of Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS), fresh coconut water, lemon water, thin buttermilk, clear vegetable soup, and breastmilk for infants.
  • Temperature Monitoring: Maintain a written temperature log and note any emerging warning signs.
  • Strict Medication Caution: Administer only pediatrician-prescribed paracetamol for fever relief. Never give ibuprofen, mefenamic acid, or aspirin, as these non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) irritate the stomach lining and dramatically elevate bleeding risks in dengue.
  • Timely Blood Follow-Ups: Adhere strictly to the CBC/platelet follow-up schedule requested by your pediatrician.

Key Takeaways for Delhi Parents

  • Dengue transmission peaks post-monsoon, but cases appear throughout the year in urban Delhi.
  • Fever accompanied by extreme crankiness, refusal to feed, or vomiting warrants immediate pediatric evaluation.
  • The dangerous "critical leak phase" usually happens when the fever comes down, requiring heightened vigilance.
  • Never self-medicate with over-the-counter painkillers or herbal platelet extracts without medical consultation.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Can a child get dengue more than once?

Yes. There are four distinct dengue virus serotypes (DEN-1, DEN-2, DEN-3, DEN-4). Infection with one serotype provides lifetime immunity only against that specific type, not the other three. A secondary infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of developing severe dengue, making continued mosquito protection essential.

Is there a dengue vaccine available for children in India?

Indigenous dengue vaccine candidates are currently progressing through advanced clinical trials in India. As of now, dengue vaccines are not part of routine national or IAP childhood immunization schedules. Consult your pediatrician for the latest updates.

How long does recovery from dengue take?

Most children with mild dengue recover fully within 7 to 10 days with appropriate hydration and rest. However, mild fatigue, weakness, and poor appetite can persist for 1 to 2 weeks post-recovery.
